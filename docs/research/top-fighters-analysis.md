# What makes the top fighting games work, and what After Hours changes because of it

A design analysis of the fighting games that set the bar today and in the past, what each one does
best, and how those lessons reshape After Hours' five fighters for a party showcase.

## 1. The games, and the one thing each does best

| Game | What it does best | The lesson for us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Street Fighter 6** (2023) | One shared Drive gauge gives every player strong options from the first second, rather than a comeback tool that only arrives late in a match. Modern controls put special moves on one button to lower the biggest barrier for newcomers. Drive Impact armors through two hits and, when it lands, crumples the opponent with a camera zoom and paint splashes in that fighter's signature colours. | Give newcomers one-button access, keep power available all match, and make every big moment wear the character's own colours. |
| **Street Fighter III: 3rd Strike** (1999) | Hand-drawn animation at a scale nobody else attempted (about 700 to 1,200 frames per character), on hardware that could zoom the camera in when fighters were close and out when they separated. Fighters "don't just fight, they perform." | Personality lives in animation: anticipation, follow-through, idle and win poses. A camera that pushes in on close fights sells every hit. |
| **Guilty Gear Strive** (2021) | Its director's aim was big damage without memorising long combos: a single well-placed hit should matter. Critics credit its accessibility to being clear rather than simple. | Short combos that hurt are more fun at a party than long ones. Make the systems readable at a glance. |
| **Tekken 8** (2024) | The Heat system rewards aggression over turtling and is activated with single buttons, so newcomers can use it. Early on it was overtuned and made matches swing wildly, and it was patched repeatedly. | Reward going in, but keep swings in check: a comeback should feel earned, not random. |
| **Super Smash Bros.** | Designed as a party game first: specials on simple inputs, so the depth is in spacing and reads rather than execution. Its creator has called overly complicated controls one of his biggest regrets. | For a room full of friends, simple inputs and instant fun beat execution tests. |
| **Marvel vs. Capcom** | Chose hype over strict balance: screen-filling Hyper Combos, cinematic supers and comebacks made it a spectator sport. | Supers should be events the whole room watches. |
| **Dragon Ball FighterZ / Guilty Gear Xrd** | Hand-picked key poses rather than every in-between frame: the studio keyframes only what reaches the screen, so motion stays snappy without "extraneous frames". | More frames is not automatically better: strong key poses, held at the right moment, read as impact. |

## 2. Principles that come out of it

1. **Every fighter has one clear plan.** The genre has settled on a handful of archetypes since
   Street Fighter II: **rushdown** (smother the opponent up close), **grappler** (big throws and armor,
   slow to get in, devastating once there), **zoner** (control space with projectiles), the
   **all-rounder**, and **setplay/trickster** variants. Archetypes are a starting point, not a
   rulebook, but a newcomer should be able to say what their character wants within one round.
2. **Hits must be read and felt.** Hitstop (a freeze on contact) freezes *both* fighters, short for
   light hits and longer for heavy ones; screen shake is matched to strength so light hits never
   dilute heavy ones; impact sounds are layered (a sharp transient, a low body, a voice). Together,
   the freeze is the connection and the shake is the consequence.
3. **Identity in every effect.** SF6 paints a fighter's signature colours on every big moment. A
   lightning fighter's hits should crackle; a heavy fighter's should shake the ground.
4. **Animation sells it.** Anticipation before the strike, a held impact pose, follow-through after.
   The best sprites start and end every move in the fighter's own stance, so attacks flow.
5. **The camera is a storyteller.** Push in when fighters are close, pull out when they separate,
   punch in on supers and KOs.
6. **Simple to start, deep to master.** One-button specials for newcomers, motions for players who
   want them, and readable systems.
7. **Spectacle for the room.** Supers with cut-ins, slow-motion KOs, crowd sound: moments people
   cheer at.

## 3. The After Hours roster against those principles

| Fighter | Archetype | Plan in one line | What changed in this pass |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The Rogue** | Rushdown / mixup | "Play smart. Strike fast." Blink in, hit, blink out. | Rebuilt from his sheet: short fade and goatee instead of bald and bearded, tattoos and earring. New special **Rogue's Rift**: invulnerable while he vanishes, teleports to the opponent (never past them), strikes, and blinks back out on a hit, an in-and-out tool against zoners. Super renamed **Financial Freedom**. His sheet dialogue, style and stats. Every hit throws off lightning forks. |
| **The Jetsetter** | Mobile all-rounder | Style and pressure: a spinning gold kick that travels, records to keep space. | Hits throw spinning records with a record-scratch layer on heavies. |
| **Chris "The Diplomat"** | Trickster / counter | Talk, distract, strike: a counter stance that punishes attacks, cards to annoy. | Hits throw his cards. He is the hardest fighter for the CPU to play (about 29% in hard-vs-hard CPU matches), which fits a read-based trickster; watch him in human play before buffing. |
| **The Leader** | Zoner / commander | Control the screen with the crew and the Golden Rule, then crown the rush. | Hits throw gold sparks with a crown flicker on big ones and a crowd roar layer. |
| **Eddnog "The Tank"** (new) | Grappler / armored tank | "Bigger than the fight." Walk through pokes and make one big hit count. | New fighter. Highest health (1150), slowest walk, biggest throw (**Bear Hug**, 150). **Armor**: his heavy (**Bring the Hammer Down**) soaks one hit and **Bulwark Rush**, a shoulder charge behind a gold shield, soaks two (throws and supers break armor, as in SF6). **Ground Breaker** shockwave, **Shield Uppercut** anti-air, and **Titan Smash** super with erupting rocks and shockwaves. Every hit throws rock chunks and dust. |

### Names changed to keep the game original

Eddnog's sheet names his special after an Overwatch hero and his super after a Marvel character,
and shows the Overwatch logo. The game uses original names and no third-party logos: **Bulwark
Rush** (shield charge) and **Titan Smash** (super), and his "Hulk Smash!" line became "Smash
time!". His look (curly hair, glasses, steel pauldrons, black and gold) is kept. Easy to rename
again if the group prefers something else.

## 4. Game feel: what is now in the game

- **Hitstop on both fighters**, scaled by move strength; **shake** only on heavy hits, scaled by
  strength.
- **Themed impacts**: every hit carries the attacker's colours and signature particles (lightning,
  records, cards, gold sparks and crowns, rocks), with the fighter's sound layered on heavy hits.
- **Blocks flash in the defender's shield colour**; **armor** answers with a steel clang and an
  "ARMOR" call-out.
- **Camera**: zooms in to 1.28x when the fighters are close, punches in on supers and KOs.
- **KO slow motion** for about a second; **super cinematics** themed to each sheet's super art, with
  the sheet's own art in the cut-in.
- **Animation**: attacks start and end in the fighter's stance; landing and stand-up settle frames;
  power-up poses at the round call and in the super freeze.
- **Controllers**: any pad, plugged in or Bluetooth, with **rumble** on hits, blocks, armor, supers
  and KOs.
- **On-ramps**: simple specials (one direction + special), combo trials in training, arcade mode.

## 5. Refinement pass (applying the analysis)

- **Punish counters** (Street Fighter 6): hitting an attack while it recovers is called out as
  PUNISH, with 1.25x damage and extra stun, so reading a whiff pays off. Counter hits on startup
  keep their bonus too.
- **Chip KO from supers**: a blocked super finishes an opponent on their last sliver, and a round
  ended by a super is called out as SUPER FINISH.
- **Hit shake**: whoever is hit jitters for the length of the impact freeze, the classic way to
  sell a hit.
- **Layered impact sounds**: a deep crunch under every big hit, a counter crack, a steel clang on
  armor, a teleport crackle for the Rift, a ground slam for Eddnog, a card flurry for Chris, and a
  cinematic boom on every KO.
- **One clear plan per fighter**: the select screen names each fighter's archetype (RUSHDOWN,
  ALL-ROUNDER, TRICKSTER, ZONER, GRAPPLER) and the How to Play page gives their plan in one line.
- **Identity tuning**: the Jetsetter jumps further and dashes faster (mobility), Chris walks
  faster, his counter stance hits harder and his cards fly faster (trickster payoff), Eddnog's Bear
  Hug reaches further (grappler), the Leader's Crew Call comes out a little slower and his health
  and Golden Rule damage drop slightly (he was the strongest).
- **Pacing check**: rounds between computer players average 32.5 s with no time-outs, and the
  median combo takes about 18% of a health bar, close to the "short combos that hurt" target, so
  damage was left alone.

Hard-vs-hard computer win rates after the pass (64 matches each): Rogue 56%, Jetsetter 61%,
Chris 34%, Leader 59%, Eddnog 50%. Chris relies on reading attacks, which the computer does
poorly; judge him in human play.

## 6. What to watch at the party

- Whether Eddnog's armor feels fair to fight against: if he walks through too much, drop Bulwark
  Rush to one hit of armor.
- Whether the Rogue's Rift is too strong against projectiles: if so, add recovery after a whiff.
- Whether Chris needs help in human hands; the CPU numbers are only a rough guide.
